# Human Factors and Trust

## 1. Lesson Goals

By the end of this lesson, students should be able to:

- explain why human factors matter in cybersecurity
- identify common psychological triggers used in social engineering
- explain authority, urgency, fear, curiosity, scarcity, and helpfulness
- connect human factors to real school and workplace scenarios
- suggest defensive habits for users
- reflect on ethical responsibility in digital systems

## 3. Key Terms

| English Term | 中文解释 | Exam-style meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Human factor | 人为因素 | Human behaviour that affects system security |
| Authority | 权威 | Using a position or title to pressure compliance |
| Urgency | 紧迫感 | Creating time pressure to reduce careful thinking |
| Fear | 恐惧 | Using threats or consequences to influence action |

### English Explanation

Many security incidents happen not because technology completely fails, but because a person is persuaded to take an unsafe action. Social engineering often uses natural human reactions:

- authority: appearing to be a teacher, manager, or IT support
- urgency: demanding immediate action
- fear: threatening account closure, penalties, or lost marks
- curiosity: encouraging users to open an interesting file
- scarcity: limited-time opportunity or limited places
- helpfulness: making someone feel rude if they do not help

The defensive goal is not to blame victims. The goal is to build safer processes and habits, such as pausing, verifying, reporting, least privilege, and two-person approval.

## 5. Real-life Example

### Example: Fake Authority

A message says:

> I am from IT. I need your verification code immediately to fix your account.

The message uses authority and urgency. A safe user should verify through an official IT support channel instead of obeying the message.

---

## 6. Defensive Recognition Pattern

```text
Trigger detected → pause → name the trigger → check the request → verify independently → act only if confirmed
```

---

## 7. Scenario Analysis

### Scenario

A student receives a message:

> This is your course coordinator. Send your LMS login code in the next 5 minutes or your project submission will fail.

| Trigger | Evidence |
|---|---|
| Authority | claims to be course coordinator |
| Urgency | next 5 minutes |
| Fear | project submission will fail |
| Sensitive request | asks for login code |

---

## 8. Explanation of the Scenario

The message combines several psychological triggers. The more pressure a message creates, the more important it is to slow down. The student should not share the code and should verify through official school channels.

---

## 9. Step-by-step Defensive Response

| Step | Defensive Action |
|---|---|
| 1 | Stop and recognize emotional pressure |
| 2 | Ask: what trigger is being used? |
| 3 | Check whether the request is normal |
| 4 | Verify through a known official channel |
| 5 | Refuse to share passwords or codes |
| 6 | Report the incident |

---

## 10. Common Mistakes

| Mistake | Why it is a problem | Better habit |
|---|---|---|
| Blaming the victim | Social engineering is designed to manipulate normal human behaviour | Focus on safer systems and training |
| Acting faster under pressure | Urgency is a warning sign | Slow down when pressured |
| Trusting titles automatically | Titles can be faked | Verify identity independently |
| Being too embarrassed to ask | Attackers exploit silence | Encourage reporting and questions |
| Thinking smart people cannot be tricked | Everyone can be targeted | Use process, not confidence |

## 11. Guided Practice

### Practice 1

Name two psychological triggers in the message: “Your account closes in 10 minutes. Confirm now.”

<details><summary>Suggested Answer</summary>

</details>

### Practice 2

Why should users pause when a message creates pressure?

<details><summary>Suggested Answer</summary>

## 13. Exam-style / Reflection Questions

### Question 1 [4 marks]

Explain two human factors that social engineering attacks may exploit.

<details><summary>Mark Scheme Style Answer</summary>

An attacker may exploit authority by pretending to be a teacher, manager, or IT support worker. This may make the target obey without checking. An attacker may also exploit urgency by creating time pressure so the target acts quickly without verifying the request.

</details>

### Question 2 [3 marks]

Explain why security awareness training should not blame victims.

<details><summary>Mark Scheme Style Answer</summary>

Social engineering is designed to exploit normal human behaviour such as trust, fear, and helpfulness. Blaming victims may reduce reporting. Training should focus on safer habits, verification, and supportive reporting processes.

</details>
