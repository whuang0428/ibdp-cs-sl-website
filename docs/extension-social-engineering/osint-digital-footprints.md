# OSINT and Digital Footprints

## 1. Lesson Goals

By the end of this lesson, students should be able to:

- define OSINT
- define digital footprint
- distinguish active and passive digital footprints
- explain how public information can increase social engineering risk
- identify safe examples of public information that may be used in targeted attacks
- explain why oversharing online can affect personal and organizational security
- recommend privacy and information-sharing controls
- explain how schools and organizations can reduce OSINT-related risks
- describe safe digital footprint review habits
- answer exam-style questions using clear cybersecurity vocabulary

::: warning Defensive Learning Only
This page is for awareness, privacy protection, and defensive risk reduction. It does not teach how to collect information about real people or perform targeting.
:::

## 5. What Is OSINT?

OSINT stands for open-source intelligence.

### Simple Definition

```text
OSINT is information gathered from publicly available sources.
```

### Defensive Security Definition

```text
OSINT is public information that can be reviewed to understand what details about a person or organization are exposed and how this may affect security.
```

### Public Sources May Include

```text
school/company websites
public social media posts
news articles
public profiles
event pages
public documents
forum posts
public directories
photos and captions
```

### Mark Scheme Phrase

```text
OSINT is information from publicly available sources that may be used to build context about a person or organization.
```

---

## 6. What Is a Digital Footprint?

A digital footprint is the trail of data left by online activity.

### Simple Definition

```text
A digital footprint is the collection of data traces created when a person or organization uses online services.
```

### Examples

```text
social media posts
profile information
comments
uploaded photos
public documents
website accounts
search and browsing data
location tags
metadata in files
email addresses
usernames
```

### Mark Scheme Phrase

```text
A digital footprint is the data trail left by a user's online activity, including information they share and data collected about them.
```

## 7. Active and Passive Digital Footprints

| Type | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Active digital footprint | information intentionally shared by the user | posts, comments, profile bio, uploaded photos |
| Passive digital footprint | information collected without direct intentional posting | cookies, IP logs, analytics, location data, device data |

### Active Footprint Example

```text
A student posts the name of their school and a photo from a school event.
```

### Passive Footprint Example

```text
A website records approximate location, device type, and time of visit.
```

### Exam Phrase

```text
An active digital footprint is intentionally shared, while a passive digital footprint is collected as a result of online activity.
```

## 8. Why OSINT Matters in Social Engineering

Public information can make social engineering more believable.

### Example Details That Increase Risk

```text
real names
job roles
school departments
recent events
teacher or manager names
email formats
common platforms
public schedules
supplier or partner names
photos of badges or offices
```

### Why It Matters

A message that includes real context may feel trustworthy.

### Example

A fake message that mentions a real school event may seem more legitimate than a generic message.

### Mark Scheme Phrase

```text
OSINT can make spear phishing more convincing because attackers can include real names, roles, events, or organizational details.
```

## 9. Public Information Risk Examples

| Public Information | Possible Risk |
|---|---|
| staff names and roles | impersonation or targeted messages |
| email format | easier to guess valid addresses |
| event schedule | convincing event-related messages |
| public photos | badge, room, or device information exposed |
| school platform names | fake login page context |
| supplier names | fake invoice or payment request context |
| job title | authority-based pretexting |
| social media posts | personal context for targeted scams |
| documents with metadata | author, date, location, or system details |
| public phone numbers | fake support or callback attempts |

### Defensive Reminder

Real information in a message does not prove the message is safe.

## 10. Oversharing

Oversharing means posting more information than necessary.

### Examples of Oversharing

```text
posting full daily schedule
showing ID badge clearly
sharing travel dates publicly
posting screenshots with email addresses
sharing internal documents publicly
showing classroom/office layouts
posting names and roles without need
sharing personal recovery information
```

### Why It Is Risky

Oversharing can help attackers:

```text
make targeted messages more believable
guess security questions
impersonate someone
time an attack
identify systems or staff
build trust through familiar details
```

### Exam Phrase

```text
Oversharing increases risk because public details can be used to create more believable social engineering attempts.
```

## 11. Metadata Risk

Metadata is data about data.

### Examples

A file or photo may include:

```text
author name
creation date
edit history
device type
GPS/location data
software version
organization name
```

### Why It Matters

Metadata may reveal information that was not intended to be public.

### Defensive Controls

```text
remove metadata before publishing
check document properties
avoid uploading sensitive original files publicly
use approved publishing process
```

### Mark Scheme Phrase

```text
Metadata can expose hidden details such as author, time, device, or location, so it should be checked before public sharing.
```

Attackers could use this context to create targeted messages that appear related to real school activities.

## 16. Privacy Settings

Privacy settings limit who can see information.

### Examples

```text
public
friends only
private account
limited audience
hide contact details
disable location tags
restrict profile visibility
review tagged posts
```

### Important

Privacy settings help, but they are not perfect.

```text
screenshots can be shared
friends may repost
platform settings may change
old posts may remain visible
```

### Exam Phrase

```text
Privacy settings reduce exposure by limiting who can view personal information, but users should still avoid posting sensitive data.
```

---

## 17. Data Minimization

Data minimization means sharing only what is necessary.

### Examples

```text
publish role email instead of personal email
avoid full names when initials are enough
remove unnecessary personal details from public pages
avoid posting exact schedules
avoid publishing documents with metadata
share event photos without visible badges or private screens
```

### Why Useful

Less public data means less material for targeted social engineering.

### Mark Scheme Phrase

```text
Data minimization reduces risk by limiting the amount of personal or organizational information available publicly.
```

## 18. Safe Digital Footprint Review

A safe review is about checking your own or your organization's public exposure.

### Review Questions

```text
What information is publicly visible?
Is any information unnecessary?
Could a message use this information to seem trustworthy?
Are contact details overexposed?
Are photos showing sensitive details?
Do public documents contain metadata?
Are old posts still visible?
Are privacy settings appropriate?
```

### Safe Actions

```text
remove unnecessary public details
change privacy settings
delete old sensitive posts
blur sensitive information in images
remove metadata before publishing
use official communication channels
report exposed sensitive information
```

::: warning Safety Boundary
Do not perform investigations on real people without permission. Keep review activities focused on your own accounts, fictional examples, or approved organization-owned content.
:::

## 19. OSINT and Spear Phishing

Spear phishing becomes more believable when it includes specific details.

### Public Detail

```text
The school has a parent meeting next Friday.
```

### Possible Targeted Risk

A fake message may pretend to be related to that meeting.

### Defensive Response

```text
do not trust the message only because it mentions a real event
verify through the official school platform or known staff channel
report suspicious messages
```

### Exam Phrase

```text
Specific public details can increase trust in a malicious message, so unusual requests should still be verified independently.
```

## 20. OSINT and Impersonation

Public information can help impersonation.

### Examples

```text
using a real staff name
copying a job title
referencing a real department
using a similar email display name
mentioning real events or projects
```

### Defence

```text
check the actual sender address
verify through trusted channel
use internal directories
follow approval procedures
train users about impersonation
```

## 21. Organization Controls

Organizations can reduce OSINT-related risk by:

```text
reviewing public website content
limiting unnecessary staff details
using role-based contact addresses
checking photos before posting
removing metadata from public files
training staff about targeted messages
using approval workflows for sensitive requests
using MFA and least privilege
creating easy reporting channels
```

### Example

Instead of publishing every staff member's direct email, a school may use:

```text
department contact address
office contact form
role-based inbox
```

when appropriate.

## 22. User Controls

Individual users can reduce risk by:

```text
checking privacy settings
limiting public personal details
not posting MFA codes, IDs, tickets, or documents
avoiding real-time location sharing
reviewing old posts
using strong unique passwords
using MFA
being careful with friend/follow requests
not trusting messages only because they mention real details
```

---

## 23. Incident Response

If sensitive information is accidentally posted publicly:

```text
remove the content if possible
report to teacher/manager/IT/security
assess what information was exposed
change affected credentials if needed
watch for targeted messages
inform affected people if required by policy
review publishing process to prevent repeat mistakes
```

### Example

If a screenshot exposes email addresses or student data:

```text
remove it
report it
follow data protection process
replace with a redacted version if needed
```

## 24. Common Misconceptions

| Misconception | Correction |
|---|---|
| Public information is always harmless | public details can support targeted social engineering |
| A message is safe if it uses real details | real details may come from public sources |
| Privacy settings make everything fully private | screenshots and reposts can still spread information |
| Only passwords matter | names, roles, schedules, and photos can also increase risk |
| Metadata is not important | metadata can reveal hidden details |
| Old posts no longer matter | old public posts may still be found or copied |
| OSINT means hacking | OSINT refers to public information, not breaking into systems |
| Organizations should publish everything for transparency | transparency should be balanced with privacy and security |
| Deleting after posting always removes risk | content may have been copied |
| Only famous people have digital footprints | most users leave some online data trail |

### Question 2 [2 marks]

Define digital footprint.

---

### Question 3 [4 marks]

Distinguish between active and passive digital footprints.

<details>
<summary>Mark Scheme Style Answer</summary>

An active digital footprint is information intentionally shared by the user, such as posts, comments, or profile details. A passive digital footprint is data collected as a result of online activity, such as cookies, IP logs, analytics, or device information.

---

### Question 4 [4 marks]

Explain how OSINT can increase the risk of spear phishing.

<details>
<summary>Mark Scheme Style Answer</summary>

OSINT can provide real names, roles, events, departments, or contact details. An attacker may use these details to make a message appear more relevant and trustworthy, increasing the chance that the target believes the request.

</details>

---

### Question 5 [5 marks]

A school publishes event photos online. Explain two privacy or security risks and one control.

<details>
<summary>Mark Scheme Style Answer</summary>

Photos may accidentally show student names, staff badges, computer screens, documents, or room details. This information could expose private data or help make targeted messages more convincing. A control is to review photos before posting and blur or remove sensitive details.

</details>

---

### Question 6 [5 marks]

Explain why metadata should be checked before publishing documents online.

<details>
<summary>Mark Scheme Style Answer</summary>

Metadata may reveal hidden details such as author name, creation time, organization, software version, or location information. Checking and removing metadata before publishing reduces the risk of exposing information that was not intended to be public.

</details>

---

### Question 7 [6 marks]

Explain three ways an individual can reduce digital footprint risk.

<details>
<summary>Mark Scheme Style Answer</summary>

The user can change privacy settings to limit who can view personal information. They can avoid oversharing details such as location, school/workplace, IDs, or daily routines. They can review and remove old public posts that reveal unnecessary information. Other valid answers include using MFA, avoiding document/photo exposure, and being careful with friend requests.

</details>

---

### Question 8 [8 marks]

A company wants to reduce the risk that public information is used in social engineering attacks. Discuss a suitable strategy.

<details>
<summary>Mark Scheme Style Answer</summary>

The company should review its public website, social media, photos, and documents to identify unnecessary exposure such as staff details, internal system names, badges, office layouts, or metadata. It should remove or reduce information that is not needed publicly.

The company should also apply data minimization by publishing only necessary contact and role information. Photos and documents should be checked before publication, and metadata should be removed where appropriate. Staff should be trained not to trust messages only because they contain real company details.

Technical and process controls also help. MFA, least privilege, approval workflows, and clear reporting channels reduce the damage if targeted messages are attempted. Overall, the company should balance public communication with privacy and security.

</details>
