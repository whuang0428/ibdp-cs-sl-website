# Pretexting, Baiting and Quid Pro Quo

## 1. Lesson Goals

By the end of this lesson, students should be able to:

- define pretexting, baiting, and quid pro quo
- identify manipulation patterns in safe fictional scenarios
- explain how attackers create believable stories
- explain why rewards or helpful offers can be risky
- suggest safe verification habits
- write defensive scenario responses

## 3. Key Terms

| English Term | 中文解释 | Exam-style meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Pretexting | 借口/伪装情境 | Creating a false story or identity to obtain information or action |
| Baiting | 诱饵攻击 | Offering something attractive to tempt unsafe action |
| Quid pro quo | 交换式诱导 | Offering help or benefit in exchange for information or access |
| Impersonation | 冒充 | Pretending to be another person or role |
| Sensitive information | 敏感信息 | Information that should be protected |
| Verification | 核验 | Checking whether a story or request is genuine |
| Policy | 规则/政策 | A formal procedure that should be followed |

### English Explanation

Social engineering does not only happen through email. Attackers may use phone calls, face-to-face contact, social media, QR codes, USB devices, gaming accounts, or group chats.

Three common patterns:

- **Pretexting**: creating a false identity or story, such as “I am new IT staff”
- **Baiting**: offering a tempting item, such as “free game skin” or an unknown USB drive
- **Quid pro quo**: offering help or a benefit in exchange for information, such as “I can fix your account if you give me your verification code”

The defensive focus is: do not be led by the story, reward, or offer of help. Verify identity and process first.

## 5. Real-life Example

### Example: Unknown USB Drive

A student finds a USB drive labelled:

> Final exam answers

This is suspicious baiting. The safe response is not to plug it into a personal or school computer. It should be given to the responsible teacher or IT staff according to policy.

---

## 6. Defensive Recognition Pattern

```text
Attractive story / reward / help offer
→ identify what action is requested
→ check whether the action breaks normal policy
→ verify identity through official channel
→ refuse unsafe request
→ report if needed
```

---

## 7. Scenario Analysis

### Scenario

Someone in a school hallway says:

> I am from the internet company. The office asked me to check the server room. Can you let me in quickly?

| Warning Sign | Explanation |
|---|---|
| claimed authority | says they are from internet company |
| urgency | “quickly” |
| access request | wants entry to restricted area |
| no verification | no official confirmation shown |

---

## 8. Explanation of the Scenario

This is a pretext. Even if the person seems polite or confident, students should not provide access to restricted areas. They should contact staff or security through the normal procedure.

---

## 9. Step-by-step Defensive Response

| Step | Defensive Action |
|---|---|
| 1 | Pause and identify the requested action |
| 2 | Ask whether the request follows normal policy |
| 3 | Do not provide passwords, codes, devices, or access |
| 4 | Verify identity using official contact information |
| 5 | Escalate to teacher, manager, or IT/security |
| 6 | Record/report the suspicious event |

---

## 10. Common Mistakes

| Mistake | Why it is a problem | Better habit |
|---|---|---|
| Thinking only emails are risky | Social engineering can use many channels | Consider phone, face-to-face, USB, QR, chat |
| Accepting a believable story | Stories can be false | Verify through official process |
| Plugging in unknown USB devices | May expose system to malware | Hand to responsible staff |
| Sharing codes for help | Codes can grant access | Never share verification codes |
| Bypassing policy to be helpful | Attackers exploit helpfulness | Follow procedure even under pressure |

## 13. Exam-style / Reflection Questions

### Question 1 [3 marks]

Explain what is meant by baiting in social engineering.

<details><summary>Mark Scheme Style Answer</summary>

Baiting is a social engineering technique where an attacker offers something attractive, such as a free item or interesting file, to persuade the target to perform an unsafe action.

</details>

### Question 2 [4 marks]

A person claims to be IT support and asks for a verification code to fix an account. Explain why this is suspicious and what the user should do.

<details><summary>Mark Scheme Style Answer</summary>

The person is asking for sensitive access information and may be impersonating IT support. Verification codes should not be shared. The user should stop, verify through an official IT support channel, and report the suspicious request.

</details>
