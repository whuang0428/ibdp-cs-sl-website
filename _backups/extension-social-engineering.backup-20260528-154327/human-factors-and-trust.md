# Human Factors and Trust

## 1. Lesson Goals

By the end of this lesson, students should be able to:

- define human factors in cybersecurity
- explain why trust is central to social engineering
- identify common psychological triggers used in social engineering
- explain how urgency, fear, authority, curiosity, helpfulness, scarcity, and routine can affect user decisions
- explain why even careful users can be influenced by pressure and context
- identify red flags linked to human factors
- recommend safe defensive behaviours such as pausing, verifying, and reporting
- explain how training and a no-blame security culture reduce risk
- apply human-factor analysis to school and workplace scenarios
- answer exam-style questions using clear cybersecurity vocabulary

::: warning Defensive Learning Only
This page explains human behaviour for defensive awareness. It should not be used to manipulate people, create deceptive messages, or carry out social engineering.
:::

## 5. What Are Human Factors?

Human factors are the human behaviours and decisions that influence security.

### Simple Definition

```text
Human factors are the behaviours, emotions, habits, and decisions of users that can affect the security of a system.
```

### Examples

```text
clicking quickly
trusting a familiar name
sharing too much information
holding a door open
using weak passwords
ignoring warning messages
failing to report mistakes
following routine without checking
```

### Mark Scheme Phrase

```text
Human factors affect security because users may make decisions based on trust, pressure, habit, or emotion rather than careful verification.
```

## 6. Why Trust Matters

Social engineering often begins by creating trust.

### A User May Trust

```text
a familiar name
a school or company logo
a person wearing a uniform
a message that includes real details
a caller who sounds confident
a sender display name
an urgent-looking system warning
```

### Important Point

Trust should not be based only on appearance.

```text
display names can be spoofed
logos can be copied
real details can come from public sources
confidence does not prove authority
```

### Mark Scheme Phrase

```text
Attackers exploit trust by making a request appear to come from a legitimate person or organization.
```

## 7. Urgency

Urgency pressures users to act quickly.

### Example Phrases

```text
act now
final warning
your account closes today
reply within 10 minutes
payment must be changed immediately
urgent security update
```

### Why It Works

Urgency reduces careful thinking because the user feels there is no time to check.

### Safe Response

```text
pause
do not click immediately
verify through a trusted channel
check whether the request follows normal procedure
report suspicious messages
```

### Exam Phrase

```text
Urgency is used to pressure users into acting quickly before they verify whether the request is legitimate.
```

---

## 8. Fear

Fear makes users worry about punishment, loss, or danger.

### Example Fears

```text
account deletion
lost access
disciplinary action
financial penalty
data loss
missed deadline
security breach
```

### Why It Works

A user may act quickly to avoid the threatened consequence.

### Safe Response

```text
do not panic
check the source
use official systems
ask a teacher/manager/IT through normal channels
report suspicious threats
```

### Exam Phrase

```text
Fear can make users follow unsafe instructions because they want to avoid a threatened negative outcome.
```

## 9. Authority

Authority means the request appears to come from someone powerful or official.

### Possible Impersonated Roles

```text
manager
teacher
IT administrator
bank staff
government official
security guard
senior employee
school administrator
```

### Why It Works

Users may feel they should obey without questioning.

### Safe Response

```text
respectfully verify
follow approval procedures
do not bypass normal process
use official contact details
report suspicious authority-based requests
```

### Exam Phrase

```text
Authority can pressure users to comply because the attacker appears to be someone with power or responsibility.
```

## 10. Curiosity

Curiosity encourages users to open or click something interesting.

### Examples

```text
unexpected file
mystery link
unknown USB device
exclusive video
exam answers
private document
interesting photo
```

### Why It Works

The user wants to know what is inside.

### Safe Response

```text
do not open unexpected attachments
do not plug in unknown devices
verify the source
use approved tools
report suspicious files or devices
```

### Exam Phrase

```text
Curiosity can be exploited by tempting users to open unknown files, links, or devices.
```

## 11. Helpfulness

Many people want to help others.

### Examples

```text
holding a secure door open
sharing information with someone who sounds stressed
helping a fake new employee
responding to a fake support request
allowing remote access to help fix a problem
```

### Why It Works

The user may feel rude or unkind if they refuse.

### Safe Response

```text
help through the correct procedure
direct visitors to reception
refer IT issues to official helpdesk
do not share credentials or MFA codes
report unusual requests
```

### Exam Phrase

```text
Helpfulness can be exploited when users bypass security procedures because they want to assist someone.
```

## 12. Scarcity and Reward

Scarcity means something seems limited or available only briefly.

### Examples

```text
limited-time reward
only 5 places left
claim prize today
free gift for first users
exclusive access
```

### Why It Works

The user may act quickly to avoid missing out.

### Safe Response

```text
treat unexpected rewards carefully
do not enter personal data for unknown offers
verify through official channels
avoid unknown downloads
report suspicious reward messages
```

### Exam Phrase

```text
Scarcity and rewards can pressure users to act quickly because they fear missing out on a benefit.
```

## 13. Routine and Habit

Routine actions are performed without much thought.

### Examples

```text
always clicking school platform links in email
holding the door for people behind you
approving common-looking requests
downloading regular-looking attachments
ignoring security warnings
```

### Why It Works

The message or situation feels normal, so the user may not check details.

### Safe Response

```text
check unusual details
slow down for sensitive requests
follow standard procedure
verify changes to payments, accounts, or access
```

### Exam Phrase

```text
Routine behaviour can be exploited because users may repeat familiar actions without carefully checking the request.
```

---

## 14. Social Proof

Social proof means people trust something because others appear to trust it.

### Examples

```text
many people seem to have joined
message says other staff already completed it
fake reviews or endorsements
group entry through a door
shared link in a group chat
```

### Why It Works

Users may assume the action is safe because others seem to be doing it.

### Safe Response

```text
verify independently
do not rely only on group behaviour
check official announcements
report suspicious group messages
```

### Exam Phrase

```text
Social proof can influence users because they may trust a request when it appears that others have already accepted it.
```

## 15. Cognitive Load and Stress

Cognitive load means mental workload.

### Examples

```text
busy exam period
many emails
tight deadlines
multi-tasking
stressful incident
end-of-day tiredness
```

### Why It Matters

Users under stress may:

```text
miss red flags
click faster
forget procedures
trust shortcuts
avoid reporting mistakes
```

### Safe Response

```text
use checklists
slow down for sensitive actions
make reporting simple
train users with realistic scenarios
avoid blaming users
```

### Exam Phrase

```text
High workload or stress can reduce careful checking and make users more vulnerable to social engineering.
```

## 16. Red Flags Linked to Human Factors

| Red Flag | Human Factor |
|---|---|
| "Act now" | urgency |
| "You will lose access" | fear |
| "I am the manager" | authority |
| "Open this private file" | curiosity |
| "Can you quickly help me?" | helpfulness |
| "Only today" | scarcity |
| "Everyone else has done it" | social proof |
| unusual request that looks routine | habit/routine |
| too much pressure to keep secret | fear / authority |
| refusal to verify identity | suspicious trust claim |

## 17. Safe Decision Process

When a request feels urgent or unusual, use a safe decision process.

```text
1. Pause.
2. Identify the requested action.
3. Check for red flags.
4. Ask whether the request follows normal procedure.
5. Verify through an independent trusted channel.
6. Do not share passwords or MFA codes.
7. Report if suspicious.
```

### Exam Phrase

```text
A pause-and-verify process reduces risk because it stops users from acting only on pressure or emotion.
```

---

## 18. Safe Verification

Verification should not use the suspicious channel itself.

### Unsafe Verification

```text
replying to the suspicious email
using the phone number in the suspicious message
clicking the provided login link
trusting only the display name
accepting "I am from IT" without checking
```

### Safer Verification

```text
official helpdesk portal
known phone number from trusted records
school/company directory
official platform typed manually
manager/teacher through normal channel
in-person confirmation with known staff
```

### Mark Scheme Phrase

```text
The user should verify the request through an independent trusted channel rather than using the contact details provided in the suspicious message.
```

## 19. Why Even Careful Users Can Be Affected

It is important not to blame users.

Even careful users can be affected because:

```text
messages may include real details
attackers may create pressure
users may be tired or busy
the request may look routine
authority can discourage questioning
people naturally want to help
```

### Why This Matters

If users fear blame, they may not report quickly.

### Mark Scheme Phrase

```text
A no-blame reporting culture improves security because users are more likely to report suspicious activity or mistakes early.
```

## 20. No-blame Reporting Culture

A no-blame culture encourages users to report concerns.

### Benefits

```text
faster response
more reports of suspicious messages
less hiding of mistakes
earlier containment
better training data
stronger security culture
```

### Good Message to Users

```text
If you clicked or replied, report it quickly. Early reporting helps protect everyone.
```

### Exam Phrase

```text
No-blame reporting encourages users to report incidents quickly, allowing the organization to contain damage and warn others.
```

## 25. Common Misconceptions

| Misconception | Correction |
|---|---|
| Only careless users fall for social engineering | careful users can be pressured by realistic context |
| Trust is enough | trust should be verified for sensitive actions |
| Urgent messages are always important | urgency is often used as manipulation |
| A senior-sounding person must be obeyed | authority should still follow procedure |
| Helping someone is always safe | help should happen through proper process |
| Reporting mistakes causes punishment | no-blame reporting helps protect everyone |
| Training once is enough | awareness needs regular practice |
| Technical controls remove human risk | people, process, and technology are all needed |
| Real details prove legitimacy | real details may come from public information |
| Refusing is rude | polite verification is responsible security behaviour |

## 27. Exam-style Questions

### Question 1 [2 marks]

Define human factors in cybersecurity.

<details>
<summary>Mark Scheme Style Answer</summary>

Human factors are user behaviours, emotions, habits, or decisions that can affect the security of a system.

</details>

---

### Question 2 [4 marks]

Explain how urgency can be used in social engineering.

<details>
<summary>Mark Scheme Style Answer</summary>

Urgency can pressure a user to act quickly before checking whether the request is legitimate. For example, a message may claim an account will close today unless the user logs in immediately. This can lead the user to click a suspicious link or enter credentials on a fake page.

</details>

---

### Question 3 [4 marks]

Explain how authority can be exploited.

<details>
<summary>Mark Scheme Style Answer</summary>

An attacker may pretend to be a manager, teacher, IT administrator, or official person. The user may feel they should obey the request without questioning it. This could cause the user to share information, approve a request, or bypass normal procedures.

</details>

---

### Question 4 [4 marks]

Explain why helpfulness can create security risk.

<details>
<summary>Mark Scheme Style Answer</summary>

Helpfulness can create risk because users may bypass normal security procedures in order to assist someone. For example, they may hold a secure door open for an unknown person or provide information to someone who appears to need help. The safer response is to help through official procedures.

</details>

---

### Question 5 [5 marks]

A message contains real school event details and asks a teacher to log in urgently. Explain why the teacher should still verify it.

<details>
<summary>Mark Scheme Style Answer</summary>

Real event details do not prove the message is legitimate because they may come from public information or previous messages. The message also uses urgency, which can pressure the teacher to act quickly. The teacher should not click the link directly and should verify through the official school platform or IT channel.

</details>

---

### Question 6 [6 marks]

Explain three human factors that may make phishing successful.

<details>
<summary>Mark Scheme Style Answer</summary>

Urgency may make the user act quickly without checking. Fear may make the user worry about account loss or punishment and follow the instruction. Authority may make the message appear to come from IT, a teacher, or a manager, so the user feels they should obey. Other valid answers include curiosity, helpfulness, scarcity, routine, or social proof.

</details>

---

### Question 7 [6 marks]

Explain how an organization can reduce human-factor risk.

<details>
<summary>Mark Scheme Style Answer</summary>

The organization can provide regular security awareness training so users recognize red flags and manipulation methods. It can create clear verification procedures so users know how to check unusual requests through trusted channels. It can also encourage no-blame reporting so users report suspicious messages or mistakes quickly, allowing the organization to contain damage.

</details>

---

### Question 8 [8 marks]

Discuss why social engineering cannot be solved by technical controls alone.

<details>
<summary>Mark Scheme Style Answer</summary>

Technical controls such as MFA, email filtering, access control, and anti-malware can reduce risk, but social engineering targets human decision-making. An attacker may persuade an authorized user to reveal a password, approve an MFA prompt, open a secure door, or send a confidential file. In these cases, the user is misusing legitimate access rather than the attacker directly breaking the system.

Organizations therefore need people and process controls as well. Security awareness training helps users recognize red flags such as urgency, authority pressure, and requests for credentials. Verification procedures require users to confirm unusual requests through trusted channels. A no-blame reporting culture encourages early reporting, which helps contain incidents and warn others. The best defence combines people, process, and technology.

</details>
